Dominican Republic Holidays from the UK: All-Inclusive Guide

How the Dominican Republic actually stacks up against Barbados, Jamaica and the rest for a UK holidaymaker.

Is the Dominican Republic Actually Worth It From the UK?

If you're the kind of British holidaymaker who's already flicked through brochures for Barbados, St Lucia or Antigua, the Dominican Republic deserves a proper look too — not as a consolation prize, but as a genuine contender. The country pulls in more package tourists than almost anywhere else in the Caribbean, and there's a simple reason: it delivers the all-inclusive resort experience at a price point that's hard to match once you start comparing like for like against the smaller eastern Caribbean islands.

Punta Cana in particular was built, quite literally, around the package holiday trade. The resort strip has decades of experience catering to European and North American charter tourism, which means the infrastructure — airport, transfers, resort standards — is mature and consistent in a way that newer or smaller destinations sometimes aren't. The Flight From the UK

This is a genuine long-haul flight, similar in character to flying to the wider Caribbean — you're crossing the Atlantic, not hopping to the Mediterranean. Direct services to Punta Cana operate from several major UK airports, run by a mix of scheduled airlines and the charter arms of tour operators, and this is one of the reasons the Dominican Republic competes so well against islands like Barbados or Jamaica: for many UK regional airports, a direct routing to Punta Cana is available where it isn't always available to smaller islands, which otherwise might require a connection through the US or another Caribbean hub. Exact flight times and which airports run direct services change by season and by operator, so always check current routes for your specific departure airport before you book rather than relying on last year's schedule.

Package Holiday vs. Booking It Yourself

Once you've settled on the Dominican Republic, the next decision is how to book it — and honestly, there isn't a single right answer here, it depends on what you value.

Booking through a UK tour operator (TUI, Jet2holidays and similar) bundles your flight, transfers, and all-inclusive stay into one price, usually protected under ATOL. The main advantages are simplicity and financial protection: if something goes wrong with the airline or the resort, you're dealing with one company, not chasing refunds across two or three different bookings. Transfers are almost always included, and customer support is in English, in your own time zone, from a company you can ring at home before you fly. The trade-off is flexibility — you're often locked into a fixed resort list, a set departure airport, and package pricing that can be less transparent than booking direct.

Booking hotel and flights separately gives you access to a much wider range of resorts than any single tour operator's brochure, and you can often find better value by mixing a cheaper flight with a resort deal from a site like Booking.com. It also lets you build a longer or shorter trip than the standard package length. The downside is that you're on your own if a flight is delayed and you miss a transfer, there's no single point of contact if the resort doesn't match its listing, and you need to organise your own airport transfer — which is not automatically included and can catch first-timers out.

If you're new to the Dominican Republic specifically, a package through a UK operator removes a lot of guesswork on your first trip. If you've been before, or you're comfortable managing your own logistics, booking separately usually stretches your budget further.

What's Different From Home

A handful of practical differences catch British travellers out, none of them serious but all worth knowing before you go. Traffic drives on the right in the Dominican Republic, the opposite of the UK, which matters if you're renting a car or even just crossing the road out of habit near a resort entrance. The local currency is the Dominican peso, but all-inclusive resorts catering to international tourists typically quote and often accept prices in US dollars or euros rather than pesos, so you'll rarely need to handle much local currency unless you're leaving the resort. Electricity runs on 110V through American-style two-pin sockets, not the UK's 230V three-pin system — you'll need a plug adaptor regardless, and if you're bringing anything that isn't dual-voltage (some hairdryers and straighteners, for instance), check the device label before plugging it in, since an adaptor alone won't convert the voltage.

If your interest goes beyond the beach, it's worth knowing the country has real depth of history and culture inland — our Santiago de los Caballeros guide covers one of the older colonial-era cities, a good option if you fancy a day away from the resort pool.

Practical tip: Before you book, check specifically whether airport transfers are included. UK tour operator packages almost always bundle them in, but if you're booking a hotel and flight separately, transfers are frequently an extra cost or something you have to arrange yourself — and Punta Cana's airport is a fair distance from most resort strips, so this isn't a detail to leave until you land.

The Straight Answer

For a British holidaymaker who already has the Caribbean on their shortlist, the Dominican Republic earns its place through practicality as much as scenery: direct flights are more widely available from UK airports than to many smaller islands, the all-inclusive resort market is mature and competitively priced, and both package and independent booking routes work well provided you know which trade-offs you're accepting. It won't suit everyone — if you want a quieter, boutique island feel, somewhere smaller may suit you better — but as a straightforward, well-priced long-haul all-inclusive option, it holds up against the rest of the region.
