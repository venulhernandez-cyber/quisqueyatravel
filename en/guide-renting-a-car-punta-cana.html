Renting a Car in Punta Cana: What You Need to Know

Requirements, road conditions, and the insurance upsell you'll hit at every rental counter in Punta Cana.

Who Actually Needs to Rent a Car in Punta Cana (and Who Doesn't)

If your trip is a classic all-inclusive week — beach, buffet, swim-up bar, repeat — you almost certainly don't need a rental car. The resort shuttle, a pre-booked airport transfer, and the occasional taxi or organized excursion will cover everything, and you'll skip the hassle of parking, fuel, and unfamiliar roads entirely.

But a lot of Canadian and European travelers come to Punta Cana with a different plan: a few nights at the resort, then a day trip to the 27 Charcos waterfalls in Damajagua, a run down to Santo Domingo's Colonial Zone, or a loop through smaller towns and beaches most tour buses skip. If that's you, a rental car buys real freedom — you set the schedule, stop where you want, and aren't stuck waiting for a group van to fill up. It also tends to work out cheaper than stacking multiple private tours if you're combining more than one destination. For a broader sense of what's worth seeing beyond the resort strip, our Punta Cana guide and Dominican Republic travel guide both cover routes people typically drive to.

What You Need to Rent a Car

In general terms, renting a car in the Dominican Republic requires a valid driver's license from your home country (most tourist visits fall within the period foreign licenses are accepted without needing an International Driving Permit, though some companies ask for one anyway), a valid passport, and a credit card in the driver's name to place a security deposit and cover the reservation. Minimum age requirements and young-driver surcharges also vary by company.

Here's the honest caveat: exact requirements change from one rental company to the next, and sometimes from one location to the next within the same brand. Don't assume — confirm the specifics directly with the rental company when you book, and again a day or two before pickup if anything is unclear. It's a five-minute email or chat that can save you from a rejected reservation at the counter.

Driving on the Right — and the State of the Roads

The Dominican Republic drives on the right-hand side of the road, same as Canada, the United States, and most of continental Europe. If you're coming from the UK, Ireland, or another left-hand-drive country, this is the adjustment to actually plan for — not just the wheel position, but instincts at roundabouts, intersections, and merges. Give yourself a slow first hour, ideally in daylight and away from traffic, before tackling anything busier.

On road conditions: the main highways connecting Punta Cana to Santo Domingo and other major destinations are generally in decent shape and well used by tourists. Once you get off those main routes — smaller towns, rural stretches, roads to less-visited beaches — expect potholes, inconsistent signage, motorbikes weaving through traffic, and the occasional unmarked speed bump. None of this should scare you off driving; millions of tourists do it every year without incident. It just calls for the same prudence you'd use driving somewhere unfamiliar anywhere else: slow down at night, keep a buffer from motorbikes, and don't assume the road ahead is as good as the one behind you.

Insurance: What to Actually Check Before You Travel

This is where most complaints about renting a car in the DR come from, and it has nothing to do with driving — it's the counter. Most rental companies in Punta Cana push hard for additional insurance at pickup, even when you already have coverage through your credit card or a policy you purchased in advance. It's a standard sales tactic, and staff are trained to make it feel urgent or mandatory when it isn't necessarily either.

The fix is preparation, not confrontation. Before you travel, check with your credit card issuer exactly what rental car coverage they provide in the Dominican Republic specifically — coverage terms and exclusions vary by card and by country, and "rental insurance" on a card can mean very different things. If you're covered, print the documentation (don't rely on having signal or an app open at the counter) and bring it with you. Read the fine print on any third-party policy the same way. Some travelers also compare rates and optional insurance add-ons across multiple rental companies through independent comparison sites before landing, which can help spot which companies tend to be more aggressive about upselling at pickup.

Tip: Before you drive off the lot, walk around the car and take photos or a short video of every side, the roof, the wheels, and the interior, including any existing scratches or dents. Timestamp them if your phone does that automatically. It takes two minutes and it's your best protection against a surprise damage charge when you return the car.

The Straight Answer

Renting a car in Punta Cana is worth it if you're planning to leave the resort area for more than a single day — whether that's a couple of day trips or a longer loop around the island. The cost usually pencils out well against multiple private tours, and the flexibility is hard to replace. If your plans are limited to one excursion or you're not comfortable navigating unfamiliar roads, you're better off booking your resort's transport or an organized tour instead and saving the rental for a trip where you'll actually use it more than once.
